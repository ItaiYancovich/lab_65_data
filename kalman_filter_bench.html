The sensor only reports position. Velocity is recovered from the correlation Ppv the filter builds between the two — which is why the band starts enormous and tightens as evidence accrues.

A better sensor barely helps here. Settled velocity uncertainty goes as σv ∝ σa¾ · σR¼ — only the fourth root of the sensor noise. Take σR from 20 m down to 0.2 m — a 100× better sensor — and position error drops about 32×, velocity barely 4×.

Nor is the assumed σa a free lunch — and note it changes nothing about the target, only the filter's willingness to be surprised. Wind it below what the target actually does and the filter's claim falls far faster than its real error: assume 0.3 against a true 0.6 and it advertises σv = 0.29 m/s while actually missing by 0.66. The self-assessment above turns red the moment that happens. What the target really does sets the floor on velocity, and no amount of assuming otherwise buys past it.

step 0

The model. The state is x = [position, velocity]. Over one 150 ms step the filter assumes the target simply holds its velocity — p ← p + v·dt, v ← v — and that everything it got wrong by assuming that is a single burst of acceleration, drawn fresh each step from a normal distribution of width σa. That is the whole physics: constant velocity, plus an allowance for being wrong about it. The allowance is Q, and it is what the assumed-manoeuvring slider sets. The real target ignores all of this — it accelerates however the world sliders tell it to, and the filter's job is to cope.

Constant-velocity model, state x = [position, velocity], scalar position measurement. Process noise uses the discrete white-noise-acceleration form Q = σa² · [[dt⁴/4, dt³/2], [dt³/2, dt²]]. The target's true motion is a random-walk acceleration plus a small sinusoid, with velocity gently pulled back toward 3.5 m/s, so the model is deliberately imperfect — as it always is in a real lab. At the default tuning the estimate stays inside its own ±2σ band about 97% of the time against a nominal 95%: marginally conservative, so the uncertainty it reports can be taken at face value. The implementation was checked by running it on data drawn exactly from the model it assumes: there its RMS error matches the σ it reports to within 1%, its NEES averages 2.02 against a nominal 2.0, and its normalised innovation averages 1.02 against a nominal 1.0 — the standard tests for an optimal, consistent filter.
